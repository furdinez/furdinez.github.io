International Political Economy

I study China’s economic footprint in Latin America and what it does to politics there.

I am an Associate Professor at the Institute of Political Science of the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, where I direct the Center of Asian Studies. I also direct ICLAC, a Millennium Nucleus funded by Chile’s National Agency for Research and Development, which studies the impacts of China across Latin America and the Caribbean.

What I am really trying to measure is slippery: what a country gains politically when it lends, invests or trades. In Latin America the answer has been less than either Beijing or Washington assumes. China filled a space the United States had walked out of, and gained influence it never set out to take. I work with observational data, survey experiments and datasets I build myself, and I teach quantitative methods. In 2021 I testified before the U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission of the United States Congress.

Books

Economic Displacement: China and the End of US Primacy in Latin America

Cambridge University Press, 2026 · 257 pp. · ISBN 9781009672245

Between 2001 and 2020 China filled an economic space the United States had vacated. The loss of US influence that followed was not the product of a Chinese grand strategy: it was American retrenchment meeting Latin American demand. I call this economic displacement — influence lost without anyone setting out to take it.

  • The argument

    Power is relational. What matters is not how much China gives, but how much less indispensable Washington becomes once an alternative exists.

  • The measure

    A new operationalization of economic statecraft that treats displacement as unintentional and takes host-country agency seriously, rather than reading every Chinese loan as a bid for leverage.

  • The evidence

    Trade, FDI and lending data for the region, 2001–2020; original public opinion and elite surveys; UN voting; and case studies of Brazil, Chile, Paraguay and Venezuela.
